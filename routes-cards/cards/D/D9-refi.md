## What it is

### Short

Replacing an existing loan with a new one, usually to get a lower rate, a shorter term, or cash out of home equity. The new loan pays off the old one. You pay closing costs again (appraisal, title, origination), and on a mortgage the amortization clock starts over, so early payments go back to being mostly interest.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Homeowners when rates fall below what they locked in. Also people consolidating debt into a home loan, and student-loan borrowers moving to a private lender. In a 7% mortgage environment like late 2026, fewer people have a rate worth refinancing out of.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

The lender earns fresh origination fees and points, and a brand-new 30-year interest schedule. 'No-closing-cost' refinances roll the costs into the balance or a higher rate. Cash-out refinances turn unsecured debt into debt secured by your house.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Refinancing for a small rate drop and never staying long enough for the savings to cover the costs. Resetting a 30-year loan with 20 years left and paying more total interest despite the lower rate. Refinancing federal student loans into private ones and losing income-based repayment and forgiveness options. Cashing out equity to pay off cards, then running the cards back up.

## What to ask

### Short

What are the total closing costs, and how many months of savings does it take to recover them? Will I still be here then? What's the total interest on the new loan versus finishing the old one? For student loans: what federal protections am I giving up?
