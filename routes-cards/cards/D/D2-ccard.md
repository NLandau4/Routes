## What it is

### Short

A card that lets you borrow from the card company each time you buy something. You get a bill once a month. Pay the full balance by the due date and you pay no interest. Pay less than that and interest starts on what's left, at a rate that's usually among the highest of any common loan.

### Long

A credit card is a loan that you re-borrow every month without filling out a form. The bank gives you a limit. You spend against it. Once a month it adds up what you spent and sends a statement with three numbers on it: the balance, the minimum payment, and the due date.

Pay the whole balance by the due date and the loan costs nothing; you've used the bank's money for free for three to seven weeks. Pay less than the whole thing and the rest starts accruing interest at the card's APR, which in August 2026 averaged 22.36% across accounts that carried a balance. That's the highest-cost consumer debt most people will ever hold, and it is handed out by mail.

The minimum payment is the number that does the work. It's usually set at a small share of the balance (commonly around 1% of what you owe plus that month's interest, with a floor of $25 or so). It is calculated to be affordable. It is also calculated to keep the balance alive for years.

![Paying down $1,100 three ways](/figures/D2-minimum.svg "Same balance, same APR, three payment habits. The minimum takes almost seven years.")

Every statement is required by law to show a version of this math: how long the balance lasts if you pay only the minimum, and what it costs. It's in a box on the first page. Almost nobody reads it, because the number that's printed in large type is the minimum, and the minimum looks fine.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Most adults. Used in full each month, it's a convenient way to pay that builds credit history and sometimes earns rewards. Used as a loan, it's expensive.

### Long

Most American adults have at least one. The question that sorts people isn't whether they have a card; it's which of two cards they have, because the same piece of plastic is two different products depending on how it's used.

For people who pay in full every month, it's a payment tool with a free float, some fraud protection, and maybe rewards. The bank earns on them through merchant fees and would rather they carried a balance, but they're fine customers.

For people who carry a balance, it's a loan at 22%, and they're the customers the business is built on. Roughly half of cardholders fall into this group in any given month. They skew younger, lower-income, and more likely to have hit an emergency (a car, a medical bill, a gap between jobs) that went on the card because nothing else was there.

The card industry has a name for the first group that tells you everything: *transactors*. The second group are *revolvers*. The marketing is aimed at the first. The revenue comes from the second.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Three ways: interest on balances you carry (the big one), a fee the store pays on every swipe (which funds rewards), and annual or late fees. Rewards programs are designed to work only if you spend more, and they're funded by people who carry balances.

### Long

A card issuer has four ways to make money, and they add up in a particular order.

**Interest** is the biggest. The 22% is charged monthly on whatever you didn't pay off, and it compounds: next month's interest is charged on this month's interest. On a revolving balance that's the whole profit model, and it's why the minimum is set where it is. Too high and people pay it off. Too low and they default. The number on your statement is tuned to the spot between.

**Interchange** is the fee the merchant pays every time you swipe, typically 1.5% to 3% of the purchase. You don't see it, but you pay it; it's built into prices. This is what funds rewards. A 2% cash-back card is handing you most of the interchange and keeping the rest, in the hope that you'll carry a balance someday and make the real money.

**Fees.** Annual fees on premium cards. Late fees when a payment misses the due date. Cash-advance fees, which come with a higher APR and no grace period. Foreign-transaction fees. Balance-transfer fees, usually 3% to 5% of the amount moved, charged up front on a "0% offer."

**The grace period as a trap door.** Here's the one that surprises people. The interest-free window between statement and due date exists only if you paid last month in full. One partial payment and it switches off: new purchases start accruing interest from the day you make them, until you've paid in full again.

![How the grace period works, and how it turns off](/figures/D2-grace.svg "Pay in full and the three weeks are free. Pay part and interest starts from the purchase date.")

None of this is hidden. It's all in the cardholder agreement, which runs to thousands of words. The design relies on nobody reading them, and on the minimum payment looking like a reasonable number.

## What goes wrong

### Short

The minimum payment. It's set low enough that a balance can take years and cost more in interest than the original purchase. Also: a card is not an emergency fund, though it gets used as one.

### Long

The slow version is the one in the chart: a balance that never quite goes away, paid at the minimum, costing nearly as much in interest as the original purchases. It doesn't feel like a crisis at any point. That's what makes it work.

The fast version is the stack. A balance on one card, a second card opened to breathe, a 0% balance transfer that was going to fix everything until the promotional period ended and the full rate kicked in on whatever was left. Each step is reasonable. The pile isn't.

A few specific things go wrong often enough to name:

- **Paying the minimum on time and thinking that's "paying the card."** It's keeping the account current. It's not paying down the debt in any meaningful way.
- **Missing the promo cliff.** Deferred-interest offers (common at furniture and electronics stores) charge all the interest retroactively from day one if any balance remains when the promo ends. That's different from a true 0% APR, and the paperwork doesn't make the difference obvious.
- **Cash advances.** Higher rate, a fee on top, interest from the moment of withdrawal, no grace period. Using a credit card at an ATM is the most expensive way to get cash that isn't a payday loan.
- **A late payment that moves the rate.** Some agreements allow a penalty APR after a missed payment, well above the standard rate, and it can apply to the whole existing balance.
- **Utilization and the score.** A balance that's a large share of the limit lowers your credit score even if you pay it off every month, because the score is calculated on the statement balance. That raises the cost of the next loan you take.

## What to ask

### Short

What's the APR, and is there a different one for cash advances? Is there an annual fee? What's the grace period? If I only paid the minimum, how long would this balance take to clear (the statement has to tell you)?

### Long

For the card you have, or one you're looking at:

**What's the APR on purchases, and is it variable?** Almost all are variable, which means it moves with the Fed's rate. Ask what it's tied to.

**How is the minimum payment calculated?** You're listening for the formula: a percent of the balance plus interest, and the floor.

**If I pay only the minimum on my current balance, how long does the statement say it takes, and what does it cost?** That box is on the first page of every statement. Read it once out loud.

**Did I pay last month in full? If not, am I being charged interest on this month's purchases from the day I made them?** This is the grace-period question, and most customer service agents can answer it.

**On a 0% offer: is it a true 0% APR or deferred interest? What's the transfer fee? What's the rate the day the promotion ends?**

**Is there a penalty APR, and what triggers it?**

**What's my limit, and what share of it is my statement balance?** That's the number the credit score sees.

And the question that reframes the whole card: *if I paid this off today, what would I have paid in interest since I opened it?* The issuer's app can usually show you. The number is clarifying.
