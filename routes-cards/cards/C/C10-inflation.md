## What it is

### Short

The general rise in prices over time, which means each dollar buys a little less. It's measured by indexes like the Consumer Price Index, a basket of goods and services priced every month. In September 2026 the CPI was up 3.7% from a year earlier. Inflation is the reason savings rates, loan rates, wages, and investment returns all get discussed as 'real' (after inflation) or 'nominal' (before).

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Everyone, whether they think about it or not. It hits hardest on cash and fixed payments: a savings balance earning less than inflation shrinks in buying power, and a fixed pension or bond coupon buys less each year. It's kinder to people who owe fixed-rate debt, because they repay with cheaper dollars.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Nobody collects it directly. But a bank paying 0.4% on savings while prices rise 3.7% is paying you a negative real rate and lending the money out at a positive one. Any product that quotes a return without saying 'after inflation' is quoting the bigger number.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Keeping decades of savings in cash because it feels safe. Judging a raise or a return without subtracting inflation. Assuming the official index matches your own costs; rent and healthcare can run well above the headline number.

## What to ask

### Short

What's the inflation rate right now, and what does my savings account pay? What's the real return on this? Is this payment fixed for life, or does it adjust? What did the same thing cost five years ago?
