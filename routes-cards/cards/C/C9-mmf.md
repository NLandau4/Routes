## What it is

### Short

A mutual fund that holds very short-term, high-quality debt (Treasury bills, bank paper) and tries to keep its share price at exactly $1.00 while paying out the interest. Brokerages use them as the default place for uninvested cash. It's an investment, so it is not FDIC insured; if the brokerage fails, SIPC covers custody, not the value.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Anyone with cash sitting at a brokerage, often without knowing it. Also people who want a savings-like yield on money they'll invest soon, and businesses parking payroll.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

The fund charges an expense ratio, taken out of the yield before you see it. Brokerages often pay a lower sweep rate on your idle cash than their own money market fund yields, and they keep the gap unless you move the cash into the fund yourself.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Assuming it's insured. In 2008 one large fund's price dropped below $1.00 and investors lost money, which is why the SEC rewrote the rules afterward. Confusing a money market fund with a bank's money market account, which is a deposit and is insured. Not noticing the expense ratio on a fund whose entire return is a few percent.

## What to ask

### Short

Is this a fund or a bank account? What's the expense ratio? What does it hold: Treasuries only, or bank and corporate paper? What's the yield after fees compared with my bank's savings rate?
