## What it is

### Short

The Federal Reserve is the U.S. central bank. Its rate-setting committee meets eight times a year and sets a target range for the federal funds rate, which is what banks charge each other overnight. In September 2026 it raised that range to 3.75% to 4%. That one rate ripples outward: savings APYs, card APRs, auto and personal loan rates, and (less directly) mortgage rates all follow it.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Nobody signs up for it; it happens to everyone with a loan, a deposit, or a job. Borrowers with variable rates (cards, HELOCs, some student loans) feel each move within a billing cycle or two. Savers feel it when banks adjust, which is faster on the way down than on the way up.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

The Fed doesn't make money off you. Banks do, in the gap between how quickly they pass rate rises to your loans and how slowly they pass them to your savings. The lag is where the margin lives.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Reading a rate decision as a prediction of what comes next; the committee says what it did, not what it will do. Locking a long-term rate the day before a cut, or carrying a variable balance into a hike. Assuming mortgage rates move with the Fed; they track longer-term bond yields and often go the other way.

## What to ask

### Short

Is this rate fixed or variable? If variable, what is it tied to and how fast does it reset? What happened to this product's rate after the last Fed move? Where does the next decision date fall relative to my decision?
