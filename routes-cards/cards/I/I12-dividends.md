## What it is

### Short

Cash a company pays out to shareholders from its profits, usually quarterly. The yield is the annual dividend divided by the share price. On the day a dividend is paid, the share price drops by about that amount; the money moved from the company's account to yours. In a taxable account, 'qualified' dividends are taxed at the lower capital-gains rates; others at regular income rates.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Anyone who owns stocks or stock funds gets them, often reinvested automatically. Retirees sometimes build portfolios around them for income. Dividend-focused funds and 'high-yield' strategies are marketed heavily to people who want cash flow without selling shares.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

The company doesn't profit from paying you. Funds built around dividends charge expense ratios like any other, and some 'income' products use options or leverage to manufacture a high payout while the underlying value erodes. Tax is the quiet cost: dividends in a taxable account are taxed the year they're paid whether you wanted the cash or not.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Chasing a high yield without asking why it's high; often the share price has fallen because the business is in trouble and the dividend is about to be cut. Thinking dividends are extra return on top of price gains. Holding dividend payers in a taxable account when a retirement account would shelter them.

## What to ask

### Short

Why is this yield higher than similar companies? Has the dividend ever been cut? Is it qualified for tax purposes? Am I holding this in the right kind of account? What's the total return including price, not just the yield?
