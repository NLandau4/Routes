## What it is

### Short

Bringing a portfolio back to its intended mix after markets move it. If the plan was 70% stocks and 30% bonds and a good year made it 78/22, rebalancing sells some stock and buys bonds to get back to 70/30. It's done on a calendar (once a year), on a trigger (when the mix drifts by a set amount), or by directing new contributions to whatever is under target.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Anyone with more than one kind of investment. Target-date funds and robo-advisors do it automatically; that's a large part of what they charge for. People managing their own 401(k) often never do it, which is how a retirement account quietly becomes all stocks.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Each trade can carry a spread or commission, and in a taxable account selling a winner triggers capital gains tax. Services sell automatic rebalancing as a feature worth an ongoing fee, when a once-a-year calendar reminder does most of it.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Never doing it, so risk creeps up during booms and the account is at its riskiest right before a fall. Doing it too often and paying taxes and spreads for tiny adjustments. Rebalancing in a taxable account when it could be done free inside the 401(k). Abandoning the plan during a crash, when rebalancing means buying the thing that just dropped.

## What to ask

### Short

What mix did I set out to hold, and what is it now? Can I rebalance with new contributions instead of selling? Which account can I do this in without triggering tax? When did I last check?
