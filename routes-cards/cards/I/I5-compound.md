## What it is

### Short

Earning returns on your returns. Money that grows 7% in year one is a bigger pile in year two, so 7% of that is more dollars, and so on. Over long periods the curve bends upward. It works the same way in reverse on debt: unpaid interest gets added to the balance and then earns interest itself.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Everyone, whether they mean to or not. Savers and investors benefit from it; people carrying card balances are on the other side of it.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

The idea is free. But it's the engine behind both the expense ratio (a small fee compounding against you for decades) and credit card APR. Anyone selling you something will show compounding working for you and skip where it's working against you.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Expecting it to feel like much in the first few years. It doesn't; the dramatic part comes late, which is why time in the market has mattered more than the size of the first deposit. And mistaking a projection ("at 7% a year") for a promise. Returns are not steady. Broad U.S. stocks have averaged roughly 6.5 to 7% a year after inflation over two centuries, with long stretches well above and below that, and none of it is a guarantee.
