## What it is

### Short

A bond's yield is what it pays relative to its price. Prices and yields move opposite: if new bonds pay more, nobody pays full price for an old one paying less, so its price falls until the two match. The yield curve plots yields of government bonds from short to long maturity. Normally longer bonds pay more. When short-term yields rise above long-term, the curve is 'inverted,' which has often come before recessions and is watched for that reason.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Anyone with a bond fund, including most target-date and balanced funds. Savers comparing CDs to Treasuries. Homebuyers, indirectly: mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield more than they track the Fed.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Bond funds charge expense ratios. Brokers earn a markup (the spread) on individual bonds that doesn't show up as a commission. Rising rates cost bond holders through price drops; falling rates cost savers through lower yields. Banks live in the gap between short rates they pay depositors and long rates they lend at.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Learning that bonds can lose money the year it happens, as many did in 2022. Buying a long-term bond fund for 'safety' when it has more rate risk than a short one. Reading an inverted curve as a certain forecast; it's a signal with a mixed record, not a schedule.

## What to ask

### Short

What's the duration of this bond fund, and what does that mean if rates rise one point? What's the yield after the expense ratio? Is this a Treasury or corporate bond, and what's the default risk? What's the 10-year yield doing, and why do I care?
