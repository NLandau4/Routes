## What it is

### Short

Spreading money across investments that don't move together: many companies instead of one, several industries, more than one country, stocks and bonds rather than only stocks. The point is that one bad outcome, a company failing or a sector collapsing, doesn't take the whole account with it. A broad index fund is diversification in one purchase.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Everyone, including people who think they're diversified because they own ten tech stocks. Also the employee whose retirement account is heavy in their own company's shares, which puts their paycheck and their savings on the same bet.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Diversification itself is free. Products sold under its name aren't: overlapping funds that each charge a fee while holding the same stocks, 'alternative' investments with high costs and lockups pitched as diversifiers, and advisors charging to assemble what one fund already does.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Owning twelve funds that all hold the same large U.S. companies. Thinking it protects against a market-wide drop; it doesn't, it protects against a single-name drop. Mistaking it for a guarantee against loss. Diversifying so far into obscure things that fees eat the benefit.

## What to ask

### Short

If my biggest holding went to zero, what would happen to the account? How much overlap is there between these funds? How much is in my employer's stock? What's the one thing that, if it went wrong, would hurt everything I own at once?
