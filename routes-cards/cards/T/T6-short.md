## What it is

### Short

Betting on a price falling. You borrow shares through your broker, sell them, and plan to buy them back cheaper to return. If the price falls, the difference is yours. If it rises, you owe the difference, and there's no ceiling on how high a price can go. You pay a borrow fee while the position is open and owe any dividends the stock pays.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Hedge funds, professional traders, and market makers hedging. Retail investors occasionally, usually on a stock they're angry at, and more often through inverse ETFs, which short for you with their own costs.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

The broker charges a borrow fee that can be small or enormous on hard-to-borrow names, plus margin interest, since shorting requires a margin account. The broker also earns by lending your long shares to other shorts. Inverse ETFs carry high expense ratios and daily-reset decay.

## What goes wrong

### Short

A short squeeze: a rising price forces shorts to buy back, which pushes it higher, which forces more buying. Unlimited loss, which margin calls make real. Being right about a company and still losing because the timing was off. Borrow fees that quietly exceed any gain.

## What to ask

### Short

What's the borrow fee today, and can it change? What's the most I can lose, and is there a number at all? What happens to this position if the stock doubles overnight? Do I owe the dividend?
