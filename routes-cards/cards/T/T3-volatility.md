## What it is

### Short

A measure of how much a price moves, up or down, over time, usually stated as an annualized percentage. Historical volatility looks back; implied volatility is what option prices suggest traders expect ahead. The VIX, often called the fear index, is the implied 30-day volatility of the S&P 500. Options cost more when volatility is high, because bigger swings make them more likely to pay.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Options traders live by it. Everyone else feels it: a high-volatility fund is the one whose statements are hard to open. Risk-scoring tools and 'conservative' portfolio labels are mostly volatility measurements.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Products sold on volatility: VIX-linked funds that lose value over time by design, 'low-volatility' funds with higher fees than a plain index, and option strategies pitched as income that are short volatility and blow up when it spikes. Brokers earn more when volatility rises because people trade more.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Buying a VIX product as a long-term hedge and watching it decay. Mistaking a calm stretch for safety and a volatile one for danger; some of the best buying days in history were the scariest. Selling options for 'income' without understanding that the premium is pay for taking the big-swing risk.

## What to ask

### Short

What's the volatility of this, and what has that meant in dollars over a bad month? Is this product long or short volatility? Is it designed to be held for a day or a decade? What happens to it when the VIX doubles?
