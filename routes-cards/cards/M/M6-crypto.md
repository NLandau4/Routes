## What it is

### Short

Digital tokens recorded on a blockchain, a shared ledger no single company controls. Bitcoin and ether are the largest. A stablecoin is a token meant to hold a fixed value, usually one dollar; since a 2025 federal law, U.S. payment stablecoin issuers must back each token one-for-one with cash and short-term Treasuries and publish monthly reports. You hold crypto either in your own wallet (you control the keys) or at an exchange (they do).

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Traders, people moving money across borders, people who distrust banks or their government's currency, and a lot of people who bought because a price was going up. Stablecoins are used for trading pairs and, increasingly, for payments.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Exchanges charge trading fees, spreads, withdrawal fees, and earn interest on the cash and stablecoins customers leave with them. Stablecoin issuers keep the interest on the reserves; the token pays you nothing. 'Yield' programs lend your coins out and keep part of the return, with your coins as the thing at risk.

## What goes wrong

### Short

An exchange failing with customer coins inside; several have. Losing the keys to a self-custody wallet, which is permanent. Sending to a wrong address, which is also permanent. Tax: every swap between coins is a taxable sale. Scams, which are over-represented here because payments can't be reversed.

## What to ask

### Short

Where are my coins held, and who controls the keys? If this exchange failed tomorrow, what would I be? What backs this stablecoin, and where's the report? What's the total fee on a round trip in and out? Have I recorded every trade for taxes?
