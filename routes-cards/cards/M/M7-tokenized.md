## What it is

### Short

Traditional assets (stocks, Treasuries, real estate, funds) represented as tokens on a blockchain. The token usually isn't the asset; it's a claim on an asset that a company holds in custody somewhere. The pitch is 24-hour trading, fractional ownership, and faster settlement. The question is always what sits behind the token and what legal rights the holder has to it.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Early adopters, crypto traders who want exposure to stocks without leaving their platform, overseas investors reaching U.S. assets, and institutions testing settlement systems. Still a small corner of the market.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Issuance and redemption fees, trading spreads on thin markets, and custody fees on the underlying asset. Some tokens don't pass through dividends or voting rights, so the issuer keeps what the real shareholder would get.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Discovering the token conveys a contract with the issuer, not ownership of the share, when the issuer gets into trouble. Thin liquidity: a price that exists on screen but not when you try to sell. Regulatory status that's still being worked out, with products that may be pulled from U.S. customers. Paying a premium over the real asset's price for the convenience.

## What to ask

### Short

What exactly do I own: the asset, or a claim on a company that holds it? Who's the custodian and who audits them? Do I get the dividends and votes? What happens if the issuer fails? What does the same asset cost in a regular brokerage account?
