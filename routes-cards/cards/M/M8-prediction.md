## What it is

### Short

Markets where you buy and sell contracts that pay out if an event happens: an election result, a Fed decision, a sports outcome. A contract trades between zero and one dollar, and its price is read as the crowd's probability. The platforms are regulated in the U.S. as exchanges for event contracts; the legal line between this and gambling is still being argued in court and state by state.

## Who ends up using it

### Short

Traders, political junkies, hedgers (someone whose business depends on a rate decision), and journalists quoting the odds. Volume spikes around elections and big economic releases.

## How it makes money off you

### Short

Trading fees, a spread between buy and sell prices, and interest on the cash you deposit while you wait for a contract to resolve. Like any exchange, the house earns on volume, not on which side wins.

## What goes wrong

### Short

Reading the price as a forecast when it reflects who's betting and how much; a thin market can be moved by one large trade. Betting on things you think you understand because of your job, which can be insider trading. Chasing losses. Ambiguous resolution rules that pay out on a technicality.

## What to ask

### Short

Who's on the other side of this, and why? How much volume is behind this price? What exactly counts as 'yes,' and who decides? What are the fees on a round trip? Do I know something about this outcome that I'm not allowed to trade on?
